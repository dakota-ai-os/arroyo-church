Have you ever broken a bone? Pastor Josh honestly isn't sure whether he has. Years ago, in a pickup basketball game, he tried to out-rebound a 6-foot-6 guy who had played college basketball, landed awkwardly on his foot, and refused to get an X-ray. His foot was purple for two or three weeks and took a few months to fully heal; it was either broken or a high ankle sprain. Either way, the point is this: just like you can break a bone, you can break a relationship, and sometimes a broken relationship hurts even more. But here is the good news: just like a broken bone can heal, so can a broken relationship. That was the central hope of his message this week, "How God Reconciles Broken Relationships," as our Grace for You series continues verse by verse through Ephesians. Our God is a God of relational restoration — not just between us and Him, but in our relationships with the people around us.

The Backstory: Two Groups at War

To read Ephesians 2:11-22 well, we need a little context. Paul is addressing one of the early church's deepest divisions: the tension between Jews and Gentiles (a Gentile is simply anyone who is not Jewish). Israel had been chosen by God to receive His promises and to be a blessing to the nations, but over time many developed a sense of superiority. By the time the early church formed, Jewish and Gentile believers often clashed over culture and theology. If that sounds familiar, it should — conflict in the church is not a modern invention. People are messy, so relationships get messy. The good news of this passage is that God brings peace to hostile groups through Jesus, and what He did for them He can do for us.

Truth 1: No One Is Too Broken to Be Restored

Paul begins not with human relationships but with the one that matters most — our relationship with God.

"Therefore, remember that formerly you who are Gentiles by birth... remember that at that time you were separate from Christ, excluded from citizenship in Israel and foreigners to the covenants of the promise, without hope and without God in the world. But now in Christ Jesus you who once were far away have been brought near by the blood of Christ." (Ephesians 2:11-13)

If you have ever felt far from God, it is worth naming why. God never runs away from us; we run from Him — often because we are running toward something else we hope will satisfy: a relationship, a substance, success, a screen. But Scripture paints a picture of a Father who runs after wanderers. Think of the story in Luke 15, where the son squanders everything and heads home expecting to be a servant. "But while he was still a long way off, his father saw him and was filled with compassion for him; he ran to his son" (Luke 15:20). God is not a reluctant rescuer rolling His eyes at your mess. He runs with joy. And He does something only He can do: He gives hope to the hopeless. Apart from Him, there is no ultimate purpose or destiny. But because God is real, you were created on purpose, with intention, and through faith in Jesus you have a greater future: the hope of heaven. None of this is earned. We are brought near "by the blood of Christ," who paid a debt we could never pay.

Truth 2: Peace With God Creates Peace With Others

Reconciliation with God naturally spills over into our relationships with people.

"For he himself is our peace, who has made the two groups one and has destroyed the barrier, the dividing wall of hostility... His purpose was to create in himself one new humanity out of the two, thus making peace, and in one body to reconcile both of them to God through the cross, by which he put to death their hostility." (Ephesians 2:14-16)

Notice that the cross does two things: it reconciles us to God, and it "put to death" the hostility between people. Why does peace with God produce peace with others?

  • The record has been ripped up. When you place your faith in Jesus, you are fully forgiven — God "remembers your sins no more" (Isaiah 43:25). As 1 Corinthians 13:5 puts it, love "keeps no record of wrongs." The trouble is, we love being forgiven but struggle to be forgiving. Some of us keep a detailed spreadsheet of someone else's wrongs. Real forgiveness is not sweeping sin under the rug or pretending the hurt never happened. You can choose to forgive someone whether they ever admit what they did or not, because your job isn't to control them; it's to have a forgiving heart. True reconciliation takes two: the person who was hurt forgives, and the person who did the hurting owns it. And as Pastor Josh pointed out, relationships are often more complicated than one person being right and the other wrong; normally both people have something to own. Once it has been dealt with, real forgiveness moves forward and chooses not to dig it back up later as ammunition.
  • Your sin has been seen. Coming to Christ means admitting you are a sinner who needs a Savior. Once you truly see your own brokenness, you can no longer look down on others; we are all in the same boat. Some people do set out to hurt others, and Pastor Josh said so plainly: that is a reality in this broken world. But a lot of the time, people who hurt us are hurting themselves, and out of their hurt they hurt us. Seeing that in ourselves first helps us respond with empathy instead of a judgmental spirit.

Truth 3: Jesus Turns Us From Tension to Togetherness

Finally, Paul shows that Christ does not just stop hostility — He builds a new family.

"Consequently, you are no longer foreigners and strangers, but fellow citizens with God's people and also members of his household, built on the foundation of the apostles and prophets, with Christ Jesus himself as the chief cornerstone... And in him you too are being built together to become a dwelling in which God lives by his Spirit." (Ephesians 2:19-22)

Christians share four things worth building on: the same citizenship (we belong to heaven), the same family (we are brothers and sisters in God's household, which is one reason the church is meant to be lived in community, not just streamed alone), the same foundation (the apostles and prophets God spoke through, with Christ Jesus as the cornerstone), and the same Spirit. When we disagree over non-essentials, we can remember that we agree on the essentials — and that is enough to stay united. Pastor Josh offered what he called a bold statement: he believes it is impossible for two Christians who are both walking in step with the Holy Spirit to be divided from one another. The trouble is that many of us are filled with the Spirit but not walking by the Spirit, and when one of us follows the flesh instead, we end up walking in different directions.

A Takeaway and a Reflection Question

Here is the question the sermon left us with: What relationship in your life right now feels completely beyond repair? Maybe it is a spouse, a sibling, an in-law, a coworker, a friend — or maybe it is God Himself. The gospel reminds us that we could never rebuild our relationship with God on our own; Jesus did it for us at the cross. The same God who reconciled you can work through you toward others. It isn't about your strength; it's about His strength working through you. You can't control how another person responds, and reconciliation takes two, but your part is a forgiving heart. And forgiving someone never means you have to go back to a person who is abusing you. Pastor Josh put it as God's invitation to each of us: come to Him with the empty hand of faith and say, "God, I cannot reconcile myself to You. I cannot by myself reconcile myself to this person. But Lord, I can't, You can. Will You by Your grace help me?"
